As with the compliance example, the sentence is not the point. This robot's
manifest composes with three substrates whose licenses are not Apache-2.0, and
the interesting question is how URML records the integration boundary that
makes each composition legal.

The manifest declares a `licensing` block (RFC-0262): Piper text-to-speech
(GPL-3.0) across a subprocess boundary, NLLB-200 translation weights (CC-BY-NC,
non-commercial) by cross-citation only, and LibreTranslate (AGPL-3.0) over a
network REST boundary with its endpoint and an opaque secret reference. None of
these are vendored, because URML's Apache-2.0 source may not pull copyleft
source in. The validator enforces exactly that: declare any of them as
`boundary: vendored` and validation rejects with
`capability.license_vendored_copyleft`.

Run it:

  urml validate license-boundary.urml.yaml \
    -m license-boundary.manifest.yaml --profile home

  urml execute license-boundary.urml.yaml \
    -m license-boundary.manifest.yaml --profile home --no-policy

Add `policy_required_max_restrictiveness: lgpl_3_0` to the manifest's licensing
block and re-run validate under the default policy: the GPL-3.0 and CC-BY-NC
components are now refused for exceeding the declared cap. The boundary is the
license isolation; the cap is the procurement gate.
