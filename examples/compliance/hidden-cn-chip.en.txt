Drive to the kitchen.

The point of this example is not the sentence. It is what the validator
catches before the robot moves.

This robot's manifest looks clean: its one critical component, the drive
controller, declares country_of_origin: US. A compliance check that reads only
the manifest's declared facts (URML's Pass 5, RFC-0004) would pass it.

But the drive controller's Hardware Bill of Materials, referenced by hbom_ref
and pinned by SHA-256, tells a different story. Inside it is a wireless module
supplied from a covered foreign country. RFC-0005 adds an opt-in HBOM-content
policy that parses the CycloneDX file and walks its pedigree, so the covered
part is caught even though the top-level provenance hides it.

Run it (the policy reads the HBOM relative to the manifest's own directory):

  urml validate hidden-cn-chip.urml.yaml \
    -m hidden-cn-chip.manifest.yaml \
    --policy hidden-cn-chip.policy.yaml --profile home

Expected: validation REJECTS with policy.hbom_component_country_denied,
naming the offending part (wireless_module, CN) inside the HBOM.

Swap the hbom_ref to an all-allied HBOM, or run with --no-policy, and the
same sentence validates and executes. Compliance is one flag; the language is
the same underneath.
